Every technology-rich project faces the same temptation: start with the
capability — the displays, the sensors, the responsive systems — and
work backwards to a justification. The results are familiar. Impressive
on opening night, tired within a year, and expensive to keep relevant.

We sequence it the other way. Every engagement begins with what we call
the human foundation: a deliberate effort to understand how people will
actually behave in and around the place — not who they are on a
demographic sheet, but what they do, what they avoid, what they return
for, and what they tell others about.

## Behaviours travel, demographics don't

Two visitors who look identical on paper can want opposite things from
the same space; the same visitor wants different things at 10am on a
school day and 9pm on a match night. Behavioural patterns — dwelling,
gathering, seeking quiet, seeking spectacle — are more stable design
material than audience segments, and they translate directly into
decisions about zoning, lighting, content and programming rhythm.

## What this changes in practice

Grounding the work in behaviour changes the order of decisions:

- **Capability follows insight.** Technology enters the conversation
  once we know what the experience needs to do — not before.
- **Content is designed with the space, not poured into it.** Functional,
  promotional, artistic and live content each have a behavioural job.
- **Operations are in the room from day one.** The people who will run
  the space understand its human patterns better than any brief.

None of this is anti-technology — our work depends on emerging
technology, and we spend much of our time prototyping with it. It is a
question of sequence. When the human foundation comes first, technology
becomes a means of serving behaviour rather than a spectacle in search
of an audience — and the space keeps earning attention long after the
novelty has worn off.
